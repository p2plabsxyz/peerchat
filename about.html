About PeerChat

Small-group chat inside PeerSky Browser. You create a room, share a key, and everyone who has that key joins the same swarm; no chat server in the middle.
No accounts. No servers. Works without internet. End to end encrypted. Messages go straight between the people in a room, locked on the sender’s device with a key only the room holds.
Everything stays local on your machine (room list, profile, keys file); except what you explicitly sync over the peer network.
You own your chats: there’s no company holding logs or resetting your password; the room key is the shared secret.

What it does (technical)

  • Rooms with name, bio, optional link, and optional picture; local profile.
  • One Hypercore feed per room; Hyperswarm for transport; live UI via SSE (receive-all).
  • Messages encrypted with AES-256-GCM before they’re stored in the feed, using a message key derived from the room key. The swarm topic is derived separately, so the value the room is announced under is not the value the messages are encrypted with.
  • Reactions: emoji reactions on messages are stored in the same Hypercore (as reaction events) and sync across peers.
  • Attachments are encrypted with a key derived from the room key and stored in a per-room Hyperdrive (peerchat-… in Settings → Archive); the link alone yields ciphertext. No app-enforced upload size limit—only disk, memory while uploading, and the network.
  • Direct Messages (DMs): click a peer's avatar to send a private message; the recipient gets an accept/decline popup. The conversation gets its own random room key, minted by whoever opens it and handed over inside the request. See below.
  • Link previews: when a message contains a link, the sender fetches that page’s title and description once and ships them inside the encrypted message. Readers see a small text card without ever connecting to the site, so opening a room never tells the linked host who read it. Turn it off in Settings.
  • Moderation runs locally on every peer, with per-room settings chosen by whoever created the room. See below.
  • Works with the internet down: rooms are joined on an isolated LAN swarm as well as the public one (@p2plabs/hyperdht-mdns, no public bootstrap), so peers on the same network keep talking. It needs a network, not an internet connection.
  • Optional Electron safeStorage for the local JSON that holds rooms and keys.

How the room key works

The key is a random secret shown as 64 hex characters. Anyone with it can join that room and read messages sent from then on; history from before a join is not backfilled. They can read history from peers who have the data. Treat it like a shared password. Use Join room with the key; for room details or keys from your archive, see peerchat-rooms under Hyperdrives in Settings → Archive.

The key itself is never sent. Peers find each other through a swarm topic, a separate SHA-256 derivation of the key, so the DHT nodes that route your lookups see the topic and cannot work back to the key or to the key your messages are encrypted with. Rooms created before this split announced the key directly, so those should be recreated with a fresh key.

Knowing the topic gets nobody in. Before PeerChat tells a peer anything about a room, that peer has to prove it holds the key: an HMAC made with the room key over this one connection’s handshake and its own public key. It is good on that connection only, so it cannot be copied or bounced back, and the key itself never crosses the wire. Builds before this (chat channel peersky-chat/1) handed the key to anyone who turned up under a room’s topic, so a room used with one of them that has to stay private is worth recreating. Old and new builds do not talk to each other, so update every device together.

How a direct message is locked

A conversation with one person gets its own room key, 32 random bytes like any other room's. Whoever opens it mints it and sends it inside the request, which only travels on a connection whose public key the handshake has already proved, so it reaches that person and nobody else. It is sent to their full public key, never to the 8-character peer ID, which is short enough that someone could make a key with the same one; while two connected keys share an ID, the request waits. Everything after that is the same as a room: the topic is a separate derivation, the messages are AES-256-GCM, and attachments are sealed with a third derivation of the same key.

Earlier builds worked the key out instead, as sha256 of the two 8-character peer IDs. Those IDs are public, they are in every member list and on every personal invite code, so anybody who knew both could work out the key and read the conversation along with its pictures and files. Conversations started before this keep that key, so start a new one for a private conversation. Both ends also need a build with this change: older ones check an incoming key against the old formula and drop anything else.

Moderation

Every peer filters independently; there is no admin and no central authority. If someone strips moderation out of their own build, your copy still filters what they send you.

Whoever creates a room picks its settings, and they cannot be changed afterwards:

  • Abuse filter (on by default): threats and targeted harassment.
  • Profanity & slurs (on by default): a word list of slurs, profanity and explicit sexual terms.
  • Spam rate limit: how many messages a peer may send in a 10-second window (5, 10 or 15).
  • Adult domain blocklist: always on, not toggleable.

Repeat violations escalate from a warning, to a final warning, to a short block from that room. The block lifts by itself after five minutes and the counter resets, so a peer returns with a clean slate instead of being blocked again immediately. You are never blocked from your own rooms.

Removing somebody from a room

Whoever made a room can remove anyone in it. Nobody else can, and there is no vote and no report queue. A removal lasts: they cannot come back.

Every copy of PeerChat checks that a removal really came from the person who made the room, against the connection it arrived on rather than against anything written in the message, so it cannot be faked by somebody claiming to be them.

What it does not do: take the room key back. Somebody removed still holds it, and could run a changed build and listen. So removal means every ordinary PeerChat stops passing their messages on and refuses their connection. It is not a lock they cannot pick, and a room key that has reached the wrong person is still worth replacing.

The three-strikes spam and abuse limit below is a different thing. That one is decided by a filter rather than a person, filters get things wrong, so it is a five minute pause and not a removal.

What is blocked for everyone

These are on for everyone and there is nothing to switch on:

  • Nudity in pictures and video: refused before it is sent, and checked again when it arrives. Covers attachments, dropped files, your profile picture and a room picture.
  • Abuse and threats in text, on by default per room.
  • Profanity and slurs in text, on by default per room.
  • Adult domain links, always, not toggleable.
  • Spam bursts, with a per-room rate limit.
  • Scam links: a link whose address is built to impersonate a real site is marked under its preview. The link still opens if you decide to open it.

Not detected: violent or graphic images, injury and death. No workable model for that exists that could run on your own machine, and PeerChat will not send your pictures to a company to have them checked. Blocking and reporting are what to use for those.

Photos and videos

The filters above read text, and an attachment is only a link to encrypted bytes, so nothing in the room ever reads the picture. Instead every photo and video you attach is checked on your own machine before it is uploaded, and an explicit one is refused rather than sent. Videos are sampled across several frames. The model runs locally and nothing about your files leaves the machine.

Every way a picture gets in is checked the same way: the attach button, a file dropped onto the chat, your profile picture and a room picture. There is no folder upload; a dropped folder is refused, so pick the files inside it. Pictures that arrive are checked as well, not only the ones you send. Someone running a modified build could skip the check on their side, so your copy has its own say before anything is shown to you. That is the same thing the text filters do with incoming messages.

Blocking and reporting

Open anyone's profile from a message or the member list to block or report them.

  • Block closes direct messages from that person, both ways, and drops any request of theirs still waiting. They keep appearing in rooms you both belong to, the same as in any other messenger, and they are told their direct messages are blocked rather than left waiting on a reply that never comes. The list of people you have blocked, with unblock, is in Settings.
  • Report opens an email to the maintainers with the person's name, peer ID, the room and its key filled in, so they can look at what happened. Nobody runs PeerChat, so no one can delete a message for you; blocking is what actually stops contact.

Using more than one device

One person can be on one phone and any number of computers, all at the same time. Link them with Backup & Restore here and Link Device on the phone. Each device is its own member of your rooms, so every message reaches all of them, and each shows your name with a fixed label: ada where the name was made, then ada@mobile, ada@desktop1. Rename yourself on one and the others follow. Join a room on one and it appears on the others.

Moving to a new phone is a deliberate step: use Move to a new phone in Backup, and remove the identity from the old phone first (PeerSky Mobile, Settings).

Checking what you are connected to

Open hyper://chat/?action=net-status in PeerSky to see every peer PeerChat is talking to right now: the rooms you share with each one, whether they were found on your local network, and whether the handshake finished. A peer with "handshake": false is connected but not yet speaking PeerChat, which is what an empty room with peers on it looks like.
